Design, simulation and sizing of a solar PV power converter system

MSc thesis, University of Nottingham, September 2026

48.05 Vsimulated output, 48 V target
1.96 Acurrent ripple, the 40% design limit
0.49 Vvoltage ripple, about 1%
27 × 500 Woff-grid PV array
8 kWhlithium-ion battery

The problem

A PV panel's voltage and power change with sunlight and temperature, so it can't feed a load or battery directly. I designed a DC-DC boost converter to connect an A10Green A10J-S72-180 panel to a 48 V DC load, then worked out how a system like this could supply a real off-grid home.

Stage 1

Boost converter

I calculated the components by hand (L = 88.05 µH, C = 36.72 µF, D = 0.235 at 50 kHz) and confirmed them in Simulink with an ideal 36.72 V source.

Stage 2

PV panel

I replaced the ideal source with a Simscape panel model and kept the converter unchanged. The output was 47.97 V at 1000 W/m² and 25 °C.

Stage 3

Battery

I added a lithium-ion battery. The calculated duty cycle no longer held the panel near its maximum power point, so I adjusted it to 0.4.

Maximum power point tracking

A fixed duty cycle only suits one irradiance level, so I built a look-up table MPPT method in a MATLAB Function block. It covers ten irradiance levels from 100 to 1000 W/m², at a cell temperature of 25 °C. I also checked the design equations against monthly PVGIS irradiance data for Portugal.

Sizing for winter in the Peak District

The house needs an estimated 5.305 kWh a day. An off-grid array has to cover the darkest month, so December sets the size. Pick a month to see how many panels it would need.

December: 0.40 peak sun hours, 13,263 W array, 27 panels

See the monthly figures as a table
MonthPeak sun hoursArray power (W)Panels

For the battery, an estimated 6.31 kWh overnight load at 80% depth of discharge needs 7.89 kWh, so I selected an 8 kWh JA Solar battery.

Limits of this work

  • It is simulation only, with no physical prototype.
  • The look-up table assumes 25 °C and can't adapt to panel ageing.
  • Household loads are estimates, not measured data.
  • Protection systems, wiring losses and cost were out of scope.

What I'd do next

  • Build and test a hardware prototype.
  • Add cell temperature to the look-up table.
  • Combine the table with Perturb and Observe tracking.
  • Repeat the sizing with several years of PVGIS data.

I've since modelled this array in PVsyst, adding shading, losses and cost. See the PVsyst project

Tools: MATLAB/Simulink, Simscape Electrical, PVGIS

Solar PV system design and yield calculation

13.5 kWp27 × 500 W modules
11,737 kWhto the grid each year
869kWh/kWp a year
86.56%performance ratio
12.3 yearspayback period

The system

Following on from my thesis, I modelled the same 27-module array as a grid-connected building system in Nottingham. The JAM66-S30 500 W modules sit on a 45° roof, with 10 in each of two south-facing arrays and 7 facing north. Two GW10 inverters give a DC:AC ratio of 0.90, and I modelled near shading using the 3D roof layout.

Energy through the year

Pick a month to see what the system sends to the grid.

The P50 estimate is 11,737 kWh a year, with P90 at 10,943 kWh and P95 at 10,720 kWh.

Where the energy goes

The largest losses in the model, as a share of energy at each step:

Incidence angle (IAM)3.77%
Inverter during operation2.72%
Temperature2.44%
Soiling2.00%
Module mismatch2.00%
Irradiance level1.87%
Wiring0.75%
Near shading0.06%

Cost and return

  • Installation cost of £14,720
  • Energy cost (LCOE) of about £0.10/kWh
  • Net present value of £8,366 over 25 years
  • Internal rate of return of 9.09%

These use the cost inputs I entered and an assumed £0.15/kWh feed-in tariff.

Carbon

The model saves a net 119 tCO₂ over a 30-year lifetime. That is 165.5 tCO₂ of grid emissions replaced, less 24.59 tCO₂ from making the system.

Tools: PVsyst V8.1.7, Meteonorm weather data
